\chapter{Introduction}
Wireless communication has become an indispensable part of everyday life. It enables mobile telephony, wireless Internet access, satellite navigation, and numerous industrial and Internet of Things (IoT) applications without which modern society is difficult to imagine. Most contemporary communication systems share the use of deterministic\footnote{A deterministic signal is a signal whose value is fully known at every instant and can be described by an exact mathematical equation.} signals, in which information is encoded by changing the amplitude, frequency, or phase of a carrier wave. Such approaches have proved highly effective, but they also have an important limitation. Because their signal structure is well known, suitable signal-processing methods can detect and analyse them and, in some cases, intercept them.

For this reason, \textbf{physical-layer security}\cite{lutovac2025physical} (PLS) is becoming increasingly important in modern networks. Rather than relying solely on cryptographic protocols at higher layers, the aim is to develop communication waveforms that are practically undetectable to an unauthorised eavesdropper. One promising approach is the use of stochastic\footnote{A stochastic signal changes randomly over time and cannot be predicted exactly by a deterministic mathematical function; instead, it is described through statistical properties.} signals. In this approach, information is no longer encoded by changing the properties of a carrier electromagnetic wave, but in the statistical parameters of a random process. This represents a fundamentally different approach from that underlying conventional wireless communication systems.

The objective of this thesis is to develop a communication system based on $\alpha$-stable noise in GNU Radio and deploy it on software-defined radio (SDR) hardware. The implementation is intended to make such a system usable on a standard SDR platform and thereby facilitate its use in further research and other wireless applications.

In addition to the implementation, the work experimentally evaluates the system through simulations and verifies its operation in an actual wireless transmission between two SDR devices.

Implementing such a system gives rise to several challenges:

\begin{itemize}
	 \item Estimating the parameters of $\alpha$-stable distributions requires numerical methods because their probability density functions generally have no closed-form expression\footnote{An expression is in closed form if it can be written using only elementary functions.} (with only a few exceptions, including the Gaussian, Cauchy, and Lévy distributions). Parameter estimation is therefore more computationally demanding than methods based on classical distributions.

    \item Developing the communication system in GNU Radio requires new encoding and decoding blocks that allow $\alpha$-stable noise to be used as the information carrier.

    \item Deploying the system on software-defined radio hardware requires adapting the implementation for real-time operation and addressing practical challenges such as time and frequency synchronisation, bandwidth limitations, and limited hardware computing resources.
\end{itemize}

This thesis addresses these challenges and presents an implementation of a communication system based on $\alpha$-stable noise. Its main contributions are:

\begin{itemize}

\item Development of an encoder and decoder for communication using $\alpha$-stable noise in GNU Radio Companion.

\item Implementation of the system on USRP software-defined radio hardware, enabling wireless data transmission in a real environment.

\item Experimental analysis of how the system parameters affect the bit error rate (BER), including the effects of $\beta$, $\gamma$, and the number of samples per symbol.

\item Demonstration of the communication system over an actual wireless link between two SDR devices.

\end{itemize}

To the best of our knowledge, this work presents the first open-source implementation of an $\alpha$-stable noise-based communication system in GNU Radio Companion together with a demonstration on SDR hardware.

The results confirm that a communication system based on $\alpha$-stable noise can be successfully implemented in GNU Radio Companion and deployed on software-defined radio hardware. The implementation provides a foundation for further research into communication systems that use stochastic signals as information carriers because it supports straightforward use and continued development in an open-source environment. This approach is particularly relevant where low probability of detection is important, for example in covert communication or critical-infrastructure protection. Its resistance to impulsive noise also makes it suitable for industrial environments in which conventional communication systems often struggle to provide reliable data transmission.

\chapter{Wireless Communication and Its Challenges}
\label{ch0}

Wireless communication systems transfer information without a physical transmission medium. They underpin mobile networks, wireless Internet access, satellite navigation, and many other technologies, although we rarely consider how information actually travels between devices. While wireless communication is now taken for granted, it is the result of decades of development in communication theory, modulation techniques, and radio technology that enable reliable data transmission through space.

This chapter introduces the basic principles of wireless communication, surveys the most widely used wireless technologies, and outlines the principal challenges faced by modern communication systems. Particular attention is given to the limitations of existing approaches that motivate the development of new communication systems.

\section{Fundamentals of Wireless Communication}
At first glance, transferring information without a physical connection between devices appears almost self-evident because we encounter it every day. In reality, every form of wireless communication must exploit a natural phenomenon that permits information to propagate through space. This can be achieved in several ways, most commonly by using electromagnetic waves, magnetic fields, or optical transmission.
Each method has its advantages and limitations. Magnetic fields support communication only over very short distances and are used mainly in technologies such as near-field communication (NFC). Optical transmission offers very high data rates but requires a direct line of sight between transmitter and receiver. Electromagnetic waves are therefore used in most modern systems because they provide a favourable balance between range, data rate, and reliability.
In the remainder of this thesis, wireless communication refers to the transfer of information by \textbf{electromagnetic waves} between a transmitter and a receiver without a physical transmission medium. An electromagnetic wave serves as the information carrier and propagates through the communication channel to the receiver. Because the wave itself contains no information, the information must be imposed on it through modulation. Modulation encodes information in one or more wave properties: amplitude, phase, or frequency. Put simply, amplitude describes the height of the wave, phase its displacement, and frequency how closely successive waves follow one another. Once encoded at the transmitter, the signal must be decoded at the receiver to recover the original message; this process is called demodulation.

Communication applications impose different requirements on range, data rate, power consumption, and interference resistance. Consequently, many wireless technologies and protocols have emerged, each optimised for a particular application. The following section presents the most widely used wireless technologies and their characteristics.

\section{Existing Wireless Communication Technologies}

Because applications have different requirements for range, data rate, power consumption, latency, and reliability, no single wireless system is suitable for every use case. Instead, several technologies have been developed and optimised for specific purposes. The most widespread include fifth-generation mobile networks (5G), Wi-Fi wireless local-area networks, Bluetooth, and LoRa systems. Despite their differences, all listed technologies share one property: they transmit information by modulating deterministic signals, with information encoded in amplitude, frequency, phase, or a combination thereof. This approach underlies virtually all modern wireless communication systems.

Although these technologies have proved highly successful, they also have limitations. Their performance depends on channel conditions, especially noise, interference, and the possibility of detecting the communication itself. The next section presents the key challenges of existing systems that motivate alternative approaches.

\section{Key Challenges of Modern Wireless Communication Systems}
Although modern wireless technologies have made exceptional progress in data rate, energy efficiency, and reliability, several challenges remain. They become particularly pronounced in demanding environments where noise is not adequately described by the usual additive white Gaussian noise (AWGN) assumption or where low detectability is as important as reliable transmission.

\subsection{Noise}
Noise is one of the most important factors affecting wireless communication quality. As a signal propagates through a channel, it is affected by natural and artificial sources of interference. The resulting distortion can cause the receiver to decode transmitted data incorrectly, increasing the bit error rate (BER).

Most contemporary communication systems are analysed and designed under an additive white Gaussian noise (AWGN) model. Owing to its mathematical simplicity, this model forms the basis of many modulation, detection, and coding methods and is suitable for numerous communication scenarios\cite{wikiAWGN}\cite{iblAWGN}.

In many real environments, however, Gaussian noise is not an adequate model. Industrial plants, power systems, and other electromagnetically demanding environments often contain impulsive noise characterised by rare but extremely strong disturbances. Such environments deviate substantially from the AWGN assumption, and systems based on that model may consequently suffer a marked degradation in performance.

\subsection{Detectability of Communication}
In addition to robustness, communication security is becoming increasingly important. Most modern systems employ cryptographic methods that effectively protect transmitted content, but encryption alone does not prevent the communication from being detected.

In applications such as military systems, critical-infrastructure protection, or certain industrial settings, it is desirable that an external observer cannot detect even the existence of the communication channel. If an attacker cannot detect the communication, jamming or targeted attacks on the system become substantially more difficult.

\subsection{Existing Solutions}
Various transmission techniques have been developed to reduce detectability and improve interference resistance, including frequency hopping and spread spectrum.

Frequency hopping is a technique in which the transmitter and receiver rapidly switch among frequency channels according to a predefined sequence. It reduces the effect of interference by allowing less noisy channels to be used and also makes interception more difficult\cite{bluetoothAFH}.

Spread spectrum expands a signal over a wider frequency band than is strictly required to convey the information. Spreading the energy makes the signal more resistant to noise and interference and permits more reliable communication.

Although these techniques substantially improve resistance to interference and reduce the possibility of interception, they still rely on deterministic signals whose presence can be detected with sufficiently sensitive receivers and suitable signal-processing methods. Continued research into signals and communication methods that enable more reliable, secure, and less detectable information transfer therefore remains important.

\section{Noise-Based Communication}

The preceding sections treated noise as one of the principal factors degrading wireless communication. Most systems therefore seek to reduce its effect through suitable modulation, coding, and error-correction methods. This approach is used in virtually all modern wireless technologies.

Noise can, however, be viewed from a different perspective. Instead of treating it as an undesirable phenomenon, it can itself serve as the information carrier. This reverses the conventional view of wireless communication: noise is no longer merely a limitation but becomes a means of transmitting information. Information is then encoded not in the amplitude, frequency, or phase of a deterministic signal, but in the stochastic properties of a random process.

This approach opens new possibilities where conventional methods are not optimal. Because transmission is based on a random signal, communication is more difficult to detect, while channels dominated by impulsive noise can also be modelled more accurately.

Communication based on $\alpha$-stable noise forms a distinct class of such approaches. The mathematical properties of $\alpha$-stable distributions permit effective modelling of impulsive noise and the encoding of information in distribution parameters. Their fundamental properties are presented in the next chapter.


\chapter{$\alpha$-Stable Distributions}
\label{ch1}
Stable distributions form an important family of probability distributions that share several distinctive mathematical properties. Their foundations were systematically established by the French mathematician Paul Lévy in 1925, and they are therefore often called Lévy $\alpha$-stable distributions.

Stable distributions, also known as $\alpha$-stable distributions, provide a natural generalisation of the normal distribution. Unlike the normal distribution, they can model heavy tails\footnote{Heavy-tailed distributions assign extreme events a higher probability than the normal (Gaussian) distribution. Student's $t$-distribution is one well-known example.} and asymmetry, enabling much more accurate models of phenomena with pronounced extreme values. These properties have led to important applications in telecommunications, signal processing, finance, and physics, where the classical Gaussian model often does not adequately describe the observed data.

\section{Stable Distributions}
The preceding chapters introduced stable distributions as the mathematical basis of the communication method considered in this thesis. Before presenting $\alpha$-stable distributions in detail, it is useful to clarify what it means for a probability distribution to be stable.

\begin{tcolorbox}[colback=blue!5!white,colframe=blue!50!black,title=Definition 1: Stable distribution]
\label{def:stable}

Let $X_1$ and $X_2$ be independent copies of a random variable $X$. The distribution of $X$ is stable if, for arbitrary constants $a,b>0$, there exist constants $c>0$ and $d\in\mathbb{R}$ such that

\[
aX_1+bX_2\overset{d}{=}cX+d.
\]

\end{tcolorbox}

\begin{tcolorbox}[colback=yellow!5!white, colframe=orange!70!black, title=\textbf{Intuition}, fonttitle=\bfseries]

Intuitively, the definition states that the sum of two independent random variables with the same stable distribution again belongs to the same family. Addition changes only the location and scale parameters, while the shape of the distribution remains unchanged.
\end{tcolorbox}

Stability is extremely important when modelling random processes. If a phenomenon consists of many independent contributions, it is desirable for their sum to remain in the same distribution family. The normal distribution is the best-known stable distribution, but it is not the only one. The following section therefore presents the family of stable, or $\alpha$-stable, distributions and the parameters that determine their shape in greater detail.

\section{Definition of $\alpha$-Stable Distributions}
Although all stable distributions share the stability property, their shapes differ. The family of $\alpha$-stable distributions is fully specified by four parameters describing tail thickness, asymmetry, spread, and location.

All $\alpha$-stable distributions considered in this thesis are absolutely continuous and possess a probability density function.

\begin{tcolorbox}[colback=blue!5!white,colframe=blue!50!black,title=Definition 2: Absolutely continuous distribution]
\label{def:zvezna}
The distribution of a random variable $X$ is absolutely continuous if there exists a non-negative integrable function $p_X$, called the \emph{probability density function}, such that for every $x\in\mathbb{R}$,

\[
F_X(x)=\mathbb{P}(X\leq x)
=\int_{-\infty}^{x}p_X(t)\,\mathrm{d}t,
\]

where $F_X$ is the cumulative distribution function of $X$. Since the total probability of all possible values is equal to 1, it also follows that

\[
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}p_X(t)\,\mathrm{d}t=1.
\]
\end{tcolorbox}

Unlike the normal distribution, the probability density functions of $\alpha$-stable distributions generally cannot be written in closed form. They are therefore usually defined through their characteristic function.

\begin{tcolorbox}[colback=blue!5!white,colframe=blue!50!black,title=Definition 3: Characteristic function]
\label{def:karakteristicna}
If a random variable $X$ has probability density $p_X$, its characteristic function can be written as
$\phi_X(t): \mathbb{R} \rightarrow \mathbb{C}$:

\[
\phi_X(t)=\mathbb{E}\!\left[e^{itX}\right]
=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{itx}\,p_X(x)\,dx,
\qquad t\in\mathbb{R}.
\]
\end{tcolorbox}

The characteristic function of an $\alpha$-stable distribution is
\begin{tcolorbox}[colback=blue!5!white,colframe=blue!50!black,title=Definition 4: Characteristic function of an $\alpha$-stable distribution]
\[
\phi(t)=
\begin{cases}
\exp\!\left(
i\mu t
-\gamma^{\alpha}|t|^{\alpha}
\left(
1-i\beta\,\operatorname{sign}(t)\tan\frac{\pi\alpha}{2}
\right)
\right),
& \alpha \neq 1, \\[1.2em]
\exp\!\left(
i\mu t
-\gamma|t|
\left(
1+i\beta\frac{2}{\pi}\operatorname{sign}(t)\ln\frac{\pi\alpha}{2}
\right)
\right),
& \alpha = 1.
\end{cases}
\]
\label{def:karakteristicnaAlpha}
\end{tcolorbox}

Because stability is not immediately apparent from the definition, we prove it below.
\begin{tcolorbox}[colback=red!5!white,colframe=red!50!black,title=Proof 1: Stability of an $\alpha$-stable distribution]
Let $X$ be an $\alpha$-stable random variable, let $X_1$ and $X_2$ be independent copies of $X$, and let $a,b>0$. Independence gives

\[
\phi_{aX_1+bX_2}(t)
=
\phi_X(at)\phi_X(bt),
\]
because \[\phi_{aX_1+bX_2}(t)=\mathbb{E}\!\left[e^{it(aX_1 +bX_2)}\right] =  \mathbb{E}\!\left[e^{itaX_1}\right]\mathbb{E}\!\left[e^{itbX_2}\right] = \phi_{X_1}(at) \phi_{X_2}(bt).\]
For an $\alpha$-stable distribution with $\alpha\neq1$, the characteristic function is

\[
\phi_X(t)
=
\exp\!\left(
i\mu t
-\gamma^\alpha |t|^\alpha
\left[
1-i\beta\,\operatorname{sign}(t)\tan\!\left(\frac{\pi\alpha}{2}\right)
\right]
\right).
\]

Substituting $t\mapsto at$ and $t\mapsto bt$ and using the independence of $X_1$ and $X_2$ gives

\[
\begin{aligned}
\phi_{aX_1+bX_2}(t)
&=
\phi_X(at)\phi_X(bt) \\
&=
\exp\!\left(
i\mu(a+b)t
-
\gamma^\alpha(a^\alpha+b^\alpha)|t|^\alpha
\left[
1-i\beta\,\operatorname{sign}(t)\tan\!\left(\frac{\pi\alpha}{2}\right)
\right]
\right).
\end{aligned}
\]


We seek to write the resulting characteristic function in the same form as that of the random variable $cX+d$:

\[
\phi_{cX + d}(t)=
\exp\!\left(
i(\mu c + d)t 
-
\gamma^\alpha c^\alpha|t|^\alpha
\left[
1-i\beta\,\operatorname{sign}(t)\tan\!\left(\frac{\pi\alpha}{2}\right)
\right]
\right).
\]

Choosing

\[
c=\left(a^\alpha+b^\alpha\right)^{1/\alpha},
\]

and

\[
d=\mu(a+b-c),
\]

shows that the resulting expression is precisely the characteristic function of $cX+d$. Since a characteristic function uniquely determines a distribution, it follows that

\[
aX_1+bX_2 \overset{d}{=} cX+d.
\]
\end{tcolorbox}
This proves that the class of $\alpha$-stable distributions is closed under linear combinations of independent identically distributed random variables, which is precisely the property from which stable distributions derive their name.

\section{Parameters of the $\alpha$-Stable Distribution}

The characteristic function in \eqref{def:karakteristicnaAlpha} shows that an $\alpha$-stable distribution is specified by four parameters: the stability parameter $\alpha$, asymmetry parameter $\beta$, scale parameter $\gamma$, and location parameter $\mu$. Each controls a different property of the distribution.

\subsection{Stability Parameter $\alpha$}
The stability parameter satisfies $0<\alpha\le2$ and determines tail thickness. Smaller values of $\alpha$ produce heavier tails and thus a greater probability of extreme values. At $\alpha=2$, the distribution becomes normal (Gaussian), with the thinnest tails in the $\alpha$-stable family.

\end{figure}

\subsection{Asymmetry Parameter $\beta$}

The asymmetry parameter satisfies $-1\leq\beta\leq1$ and determines the magnitude and direction of asymmetry. The distribution is symmetric at $\beta=0$; positive values produce a more pronounced right tail, whereas negative values produce a more pronounced left tail.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{beta_parameter.png}
    \caption{Distributions for different values of $\beta$ \cite{wikiStableDistribution}.}
    \label{fig:wikiStableBeta}
\end{figure}

The effect of negative values of $\beta$ is obtained by reflecting the curves shown in the figure.

\subsection{Scale Parameter $\gamma$}

The scale parameter satisfies $\gamma>0$ and determines the spread or scale of the distribution. Larger values produce a wider distribution. Its role resembles that of the standard deviation of a normal distribution, but the quantities are not identical because most $\alpha$-stable distributions do not have finite variance.

\subsection{Location Parameter $\mu$}

The location parameter may take any real value, $\mu\in\mathbb{R}$, and determines the distribution's position on the real axis. Changing $\mu$ shifts the entire distribution left or right without affecting its shape or spread.

\subsection{Normal (Gaussian) Distribution}
At $\alpha=2$, the result is the normal distribution, the only $\alpha$-stable distribution with finite variance. Its density is symmetric and its tails are the thinnest within the $\alpha$-stable family.

Its probability density can be written in closed form:

\[
f(x)=
\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}}
\exp\left(
-\frac{(x-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}
\right),
\]

where $\mu$ is the expected value and $\sigma^2$ is the variance.

    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{normalna_porazdelitev.png}
    \caption{Probability densities of normal distributions for different parameter values \cite{wikiNormalDistribution}.}
    \label{fig:normalna}
\end{figure}


\subsection{Cauchy Distribution}
The Cauchy distribution is the special case $\alpha=1$ and $\beta=0$. It is symmetric with very heavy tails and consequently has neither a defined expected value nor a finite variance. It is often used as an example of a distribution with a pronounced probability of extreme values.

Its density is

\[
f(x)=
\frac{1}{\pi}
\frac{\gamma}
{{(x-x_{0})}^2+\gamma^2},
\]

where $x_0$ determines the centre of the distribution and $\gamma$ determines its spread.

    \centering

    \label{fig:cauchy}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Lévy Distribution}

The Lévy distribution is one of the most asymmetric $\alpha$-stable distributions and corresponds to $\alpha=\frac{1}{2}$ and $\beta=1$. Its very heavy right tail makes it suitable for processes in which rare but extremely large deviations may occur.

Its density is

\[
f(x)=
\sqrt{\frac{c}{2\pi}}
\frac{
\exp\left(-\frac{c}{2(x-\mu)}\right)
}
{(x-\mu)^{3/2}},
\qquad x>\mu .
\]

Here, $\mu$ is the location parameter that shifts the distribution along the real axis, while $c$ is the scale parameter that controls its spread.

\section{Generalised Central Limit Theorem}


The central limit theorem is one of the most important results in probability theory. It describes the behaviour of sums of many independent random variables.
\begin{tcolorbox}[colback=blue!5!white,colframe=blue!50!black,title=Definition 5: Central limit theorem (CLT)]
\label{def:CLI}
Let $X_1,X_2,\dots,X_n$ be independent and identically distributed random variables with finite expected value

\[
\mathbb{E}[X_i]=\mu
\]

and finite variance

\[
\operatorname{Var}(X_i)=\sigma^2<\infty.
\]

Then

\[
\frac{X_1+\cdots+X_n-n\mu}{\sigma\sqrt{n}}
\overset{d}{\longrightarrow}
N(0,1),
\]

where $\overset{d}{\longrightarrow}$ denotes convergence in distribution.
\end{tcolorbox}
\begin{tcolorbox}[colback=yellow!5!white, colframe=orange!70!black, title=\textbf{Intuition}, fonttitle=\bfseries]
The cumulative distribution function of the normalised sum approaches that of the standard normal distribution. Importantly, the individual random variables need not themselves be normally distributed. This helps explain why the normal distribution occurs so frequently in nature and everyday phenomena.
\end{tcolorbox}

The connection between the central limit theorem and $\alpha$-stable distributions lies in their stability under the addition of independent random variables. The normal distribution is the special $\alpha$-stable case

\[
\alpha=2.
\]

For other parameter values,

\[
0<\alpha<2
\]

$\alpha$-stable distributions have infinite variance, and for $\alpha\leq1$ they do not possess a finite expected value either. The assumptions of the classical central limit theorem, which requires a finite expected value and variance, are therefore not satisfied. The generalised central limit theorem shows that appropriately normalised sums of such random variables converge not to a normal distribution but to an $\alpha$-stable distribution. In this sense, $\alpha$-stable distributions provide a natural generalisation of the classical central limit theorem.

This connection is formalised by the generalised central limit theorem.

\begin{tcolorbox}[colback=blue!5!white,colframe=blue!50!black, title=Definition 6: Generalised central limit theorem]
\label{def:GCLI}
Let $Z$ be a random variable. Its distribution is stable if and only if there exist independent and identically distributed random variables

\[
X_1,X_2,X_3,\dots
\]

and constants

\[
a_n>0,\qquad b_n\in\mathbb{R},
\]

such that

\[
a_n(X_1+\cdots+X_n)-b_n
\overset{d}{\longrightarrow}
Z.
\]
\end{tcolorbox}
\begin{tcolorbox}[colback=yellow!5!white, colframe=orange!70!black, title=\textbf{Intuition}, fonttitle=\bfseries]
If the sum of many independent and identically distributed random variables converges to a non-degenerate distribution after suitable scaling and shifting, that limiting distribution must be stable.
\end{tcolorbox}

Finite variance is the key condition under which the classical central limit theorem yields a normal distribution. The generalised theorem extends this result to distributions with infinite variance. In that case, the limiting distribution is $\alpha$-stable, and $\alpha$ determines the scaling rate of the sum:

\[
a_n \propto n^{-1/\alpha}.
\]
This is the generalised scaling factor. In the classical theorem, the factor is $\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}$; substituting $\alpha=2$ recovers exactly this expression.

Consequently, $\alpha$-stable distributions are the only possible non-degenerate limiting distributions for suitably normalised sums of many independent and identically distributed random variables. The theorem explains why they are appropriate for signals formed from many random contributions: stability ensures that the statistical form of the signal is preserved when independent components are combined.

\section{Significance of Stability for Communication-Channel Modelling}
In a communication system, the signal received is generally not identical to the transmitted signal. During transmission it is affected by numerous independent random phenomena, including electromagnetic interference, multipath propagation, interference from other users, and noise from electronic components. The received signal is therefore often a sum of many random contributions.

It is therefore desirable for a mathematical channel model to remain in the same distribution family when independent components are added. This is precisely what stability provides; see Definitions~\ref{def:stable} and~\ref{def:GCLI}.

Mathematically, if the individual random contributions follow stable distributions, their sum again belongs to the same family. Only the parameters may change; the basic distributional form remains unchanged. This substantially simplifies system analysis because a single family can describe the complete channel regardless of the number of contributions forming the received signal.

Conventional systems usually assume that the individual contributions have finite variance. By the central limit theorem~\ref{def:CLI}, the sum of many such contributions converges to a normal distribution, which explains the success of the AWGN model in many settings.

In many real environments, however, this assumption is not satisfied. Impulsive noise contains rare but pronounced deviations and may not have finite variance. In such cases, the classical Gaussian model no longer adequately describes the channel.

This is where $\alpha$-stable distributions become important. Stability keeps sums of many impulsive contributions within the same distribution family, enabling substantially more accurate models of channels dominated by impulsive noise. Beyond noise modelling, the property also suggests a fundamentally different communication method: rather than treating noise as undesirable, its statistical properties can be used to carry information. The communication method investigated in this thesis is based on this idea.



\chapter{Communication Using $\alpha$-Stable Noise}
The preceding chapter presented the mathematical properties of $\alpha$-stable distributions. This chapter explains how those properties can be used to transmit information.

Conventional communication systems encode information in deterministic properties of a carrier, such as amplitude, phase, or frequency. In $\alpha$-stable communication, information is represented not by the signal waveform but by the statistical parameters of its distribution.

Rather than specifying the instantaneous signal value, the transmitter generates a sequence of random samples from an $\alpha$-stable distribution,

\[
X_1,X_2,\ldots,X_n
\sim
S_\alpha(\beta,\gamma,\delta),
\]

where the selected parameters represent the information to be transmitted.

\section{Encoding Information}

In communication using $\alpha$-stable noise, information is represented not by an individual signal value but by parameters of the $\alpha$-stable distribution from which the signal is generated.

\begin{tcolorbox}[colback=blue!5!white,colframe=blue!50!black, title=Definition 7: Theoretical operation of the transmitter]
Let $N$ be the number of samples representing one symbol. For each symbol, the transmitter generates the sequence
\[
X_1,X_2,\dots,X_N
\sim
S_\alpha(\beta, \gamma,\mu)
\],
where the selected distribution parameters determine the transmitted symbol.
\end{tcolorbox}

An individual sample does not contain sufficient information about the underlying distribution because every sample is random. Only a sequence of samples reveals the distribution's statistical properties. Each symbol, or bit sequence, is therefore represented by $N$ consecutive samples. Increasing $N$ generally improves parameter estimation and reduces errors, but also increases the transmission time of each symbol.

Digital transmission requires a mapping between binary symbols and distribution parameters. Each input-bit combination is assigned a combination of $\alpha$, $\beta$, $\gamma$, and $\mu$. For each symbol, the transmitter selects the appropriate parameters and generates $N$ samples, which the decoder subsequently receives and decodes.

\section{Decoding Information}

After transmission through the channel, the decoder receives $N$ consecutive samples for each symbol. Its objective is to determine which $\alpha$-stable distribution parameters were used to generate the sequence.

\begin{tcolorbox}[colback=blue!5!white,colframe=blue!50!black, title=Definition 8: Theoretical operation of the receiver]
Let $N$ be the number of samples representing one symbol. For each symbol, the receiver obtains the sequence
\[
Y_1,Y_2,\dots,Y_N
\],
where
\[
Y_i = X_i + W_i, \qquad i=1,\ldots,N,
\]

where $X_i$ is the transmitted sample and $W_i$ is channel noise.
\end{tcolorbox}

\begin{tcolorbox}[colback=purple!5!white,colframe=purple!50!black,
title=Communication example]
\label{komunikacija_primer}
Assume that information is encoded only through $\beta$, with $\beta=-1$ representing bit 0 and $\beta=1$ representing bit 1. For each bit, the transmitter generates $N$ samples from the corresponding $\alpha$-stable distribution. The receiver estimates $\beta$ from the received samples. A positive estimate is decoded as bit 1; otherwise, the symbol is decoded as bit 0.

\end{tcolorbox}


Decoding accuracy primarily depends on parameter-estimation quality. A larger $N$ generally provides a better estimate and therefore more reliable recovery of the information.

\section{Why Does the Communication Method Work?}

Communication using $\alpha$-stable noise relies on the mathematical properties introduced above. The key results are distributional stability~\ref{def:stable} and the generalised central limit theorem~\ref{def:GCLI}.

Stability ensures that a sum of independent $\alpha$-stable random variables remains $\alpha$-stable. The statistical structure is consequently preserved during generation and transmission, and the information remains encoded in the distribution parameters rather than in any individual sample.

The generalised central limit theorem explains why distribution parameters can be estimated from a sample sequence. As $N$ increases, the observed statistical properties become increasingly representative of the source distribution, and parameter estimates become more accurate.

If sufficiently distinct parameter combinations are assigned to different symbols, the estimated parameters can reliably distinguish them. A larger sample count therefore generally reduces the probability of an incorrect decision and improves transmission reliability.

\chapter{System Design and Implementation}

This chapter presents the design and implementation of the developed communication system. It first outlines the development environment and system architecture, then describes the encoder and decoder, and finally presents the adaptations required to transfer the system from simulation to SDR hardware.

\section{Development Environment}

Software-defined radio (SDR) is a radio platform in which a large portion of signal processing is performed in software. The hardware primarily converts between analogue and digital signals and transmits or receives radio-frequency signals, while operations such as filtering, modulation, and demodulation run on a computer. The communication system can therefore be modified and tested without changing the radio hardware design.

In this thesis, SDR is used to transfer the developed system from simulation to an actual wireless link. The system could first be developed and evaluated in a controlled software environment and then verified through transmission between two radio devices. The fundamental encoding and decoding logic remained unchanged, while the hardware implementation required additional adaptations for continuous sample processing and synchronisation.

The system was developed in the open-source GNU Radio environment for processing data streams and digital signals. A system is assembled from interconnected functional blocks that exchange samples or other data. Each block performs a specific task, such as reading input, generating a signal, modelling a channel, or processing received samples. This block-based design allows individual components to be developed, tested, and replaced independently of the rest of the flowgraph.

GNU Radio Companion (GRC) was used to assemble and configure the blocks graphically. It represents the communication system as a flowgraph in which connections determine the direction of data transfer and block parameters determine processing behaviour. This view makes the complete system easier to inspect and permits rapid changes to simulation settings.

The encoder and decoder developed for this thesis are custom Python blocks because the required processing of $\alpha$-stable distributions is not directly available in the standard GNU Radio blocks. The remaining components use existing blocks for reading and writing data, adding noise, and interfacing with radio hardware. The same environment could therefore be used both for simulation and for preparing the SDR implementation.

\section{Communication-System Architecture}

The developed system transmits information through the statistical properties of $\alpha$-stable distributions. Its basic flow consists of a data source, encoder, communication channel, decoder, and data sink. Figure~\ref{GNU} shows the block diagram.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{Alpha_encoder and decoder.png}
\caption{Block diagram of the communication system.}
\label{GNU}

The data source produces a bit or data sequence and forwards it to the encoder. Based on the bit-to-symbol mapping, the encoder assigns appropriate $\alpha$-stable parameters to every symbol and generates samples from the selected distribution.

The generated samples pass through the communication channel. In simulation, the channel is modelled in software and can include disturbances such as Gaussian noise; with SDR equipment, it is an actual radio link.

At the receiver, the decoder estimates the $\alpha$-stable parameters from the received samples and uses them to determine the corresponding symbols or bits. The decoded data are passed to the sink, where they are reassembled into the output.

The following sections describe the encoder and decoder implementation in detail.


\section{Encoder Implementation}

The encoder converts an input bit sequence into a signal whose statistical properties represent the information. Unlike conventional systems, information is encoded not in the amplitude, phase, or frequency of a carrier, but in the parameters of an $\alpha$-stable distribution.

The encoder receives a byte stream, converts it into individual bits, and stores them in a buffer. The number of bits required for one symbol is then read from that buffer.

\subsection{Mapping Bits to Parameters}

The number of bits represented by a symbol depends on the number of available parameter values. For each parameter, the number of possible values must be a power of two so that bit combinations map uniquely to parameter values. The number of bits encoded by one parameter is

$n = \log_2(M)$

where $M$ is the number of possible values. The total number of bits represented by a symbol is the sum of the numbers encoded by the individual parameters.

\subsection{Mapping Symbols to Distribution Parameters}
The implementation supports encoding information in $\alpha$, $\beta$, and $\gamma$. In all simulations presented in this thesis, information is carried by $\beta$, while $\alpha$ and $\gamma$ remain constant.

The input-bit combination determines an index in a mapping table from which the corresponding value of $\beta$ is selected. Each symbol therefore represents a particular $\alpha$-stable distribution.

\subsection{Sample Generation}
For each symbol, the encoder generates $N$ samples from the $\alpha$-stable distribution, where $N$ corresponds to the variable $\texttt{samples\_per\_symbol}$. These samples form the encoder output and are passed to the communication channel or the next GRC block.

\subsection{Chambers--Mallows--Stuck Algorithm}
The signal is generated using the Chambers--Mallows--Stuck (CMS) algorithm,
\label{CMS}
\begin{equation}
X=\gamma C
\frac{\sin\left(\alpha V+B\right)}
{\left(\cos V\right)^{1/\alpha}}
\left(
\frac{\cos\left((1-\alpha)V-B\right)}
{W}
\right)^{\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}},
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}
B=\arctan\left(\beta\tan\frac{\pi\alpha}{2}\right),
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
C=\left(1+\beta^2\tan^2\frac{\pi\alpha}{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2\alpha}}.
\end{equation}

Here, $V$ is uniformly distributed on \begin{equation} V\sim\mathcal{U}\left(-\frac{\pi}{2},\frac{\pi}{2}\right), \end{equation} while $W$ is exponentially distributed with unit mean, \begin{equation} W\sim\mathrm{Exp}(1). \end{equation}

For every input symbol, the encoder thus produces $N$ samples whose distribution is determined by the selected values of $\alpha$, $\beta$, and $\gamma$.

\section{Decoder Implementation}
The decoder estimates the $\alpha$-stable parameters from the received samples and reconstructs the original bit sequence. Because one sample is insufficient for reliable estimation, the decoder analyses several samples together.

\subsection{Dividing a Symbol into Segments}
The decoder stores the incoming sample stream in a buffer. Since the encoder generates exactly $N$ samples for every symbol, individual symbols are separated by processing each successive group of $N$ samples.

\subsection{Estimating the Parameter $\beta$}
The samples of one symbol are divided into $L$ equal segments, each containing $K=N/L$ samples. For each segment, the maximum and minimum are found and their magnitudes are transformed logarithmically:

\begin{equation}
Y_{l,\max}=\ln\!\left(\max_{1\leq k\leq K} X_{l,k}\right),
\qquad
Y_{l,\min}=\ln\!\left(-\min_{1\leq k\leq K} X_{l,k}\right).
\end{equation}

The means and variances are then calculated from the logarithmic extrema:

\begin{equation}
Y_{\max}=\frac{1}{L}\sum_{l=1}^{L}Y_{l,\max},
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
S^2_{\max}=\frac{1}{L-1}\sum_{l=1}^{L}(Y_{l,\max}-Y_{\max})^2,
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
Y_{\min}=\frac{1}{L}\sum_{l=1}^{L}Y_{l,\min},
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
S^2_{\min}=\frac{1}{L-1}\sum_{l=1}^{L}(Y_{l,\min}-Y_{\min})^2.
\end{equation}
Here, $S_{\max}$ and $S_{\min}$ are the standard deviations, i.e., the positive square roots of the corresponding variances. They are first used to estimate $\alpha$:

\begin{equation}
\hat{\alpha}
=
\frac{\pi}{2\sqrt{6}}
\left(
\frac{1}{S_{\max}}
+
\frac{1}{S_{\min}}
\right).
\end{equation}

The parameter $\beta$ is then estimated using the hyperbolic tangent:

\begin{equation}
\hat{\beta}
=
\tanh\!\left(
\frac{\hat{\alpha}}{2}
(Y_{\max}-Y_{\min})
\right).
\end{equation}

The same estimator is also encountered in the equivalent exponential form

\begin{equation}
\hat{\beta}
=
1-
\frac{2}
{\exp\!\left(\hat{\alpha}(Y_{\max}-Y_{\min})\right)+1}.
\end{equation}

The following proof establishes the equivalence of the two forms.

\begin{tcolorbox}[colback=red!5!white,colframe=red!50!black,title=Proof 2: Equivalence of the two forms of the $\beta$ estimator]
Let
\[
z=\hat{\alpha}(Y_{\max}-Y_{\min}).
\]
The definition of the hyperbolic tangent gives
\[
\begin{aligned}
\tanh\!\left(\frac{z}{2}\right)
&=
\frac{\exp(z/2)-\exp(-z/2)}
{\exp(z/2)+\exp(-z/2)} \\
&=
\frac{\exp(z)-1}{\exp(z)+1} \\
&=
1-
\frac{2}{\exp(z)+1}.
\end{aligned}
\]

Substituting $z=\hat{\alpha}(Y_{\max}-Y_{\min})$ recovers the stated exponential form.
\end{tcolorbox}
Thus, the hyperbolic-tangent and exponential forms of the $\beta$ estimator are equivalent.

\subsection{Bit Decoding}
The estimate $\hat{\beta}$ is compared with the permitted values in the mapping table. The nearest value is selected, and its index represents the decoded bit combination. Decoded bits are buffered until eight are available, after which the decoder combines them into one byte and sends it to the output.

\section{Implementation on SDR Hardware}

Transferring the system from simulation to physical hardware introduces constraints that need not be considered in simulation. The most important are real-time sample delivery and synchronisation between transmitter and receiver.

In simulation, the sample-generation rate was not critical and the signal start was known exactly. A physical receiver, however, samples continuously, so the stream before a packet contains noise, interference, and possibly other environmental signals. The receiver must therefore determine the packet start autonomously.

An SDR transmitter must receive complex samples at the required sampling rate. If the computer cannot supply them quickly enough, an \texttt{underflow} occurs: the device temporarily runs out of new samples, potentially interrupting the signal and causing decoding errors. Since generating $\alpha$-stable samples is more computationally demanding than generating simple deterministic signals, the generation method had to be adapted for real-time operation.

The following subsections present vectorised $\alpha$-stable sample generation and the synchronisation procedure used between two SDR devices.

\subsection{Vectorised Generation of $\alpha$-Stable Samples}

In the original simulation implementation, $\alpha$-stable samples could be generated sequentially because execution time did not affect transmission continuity. This is unsuitable for SDR hardware, where samples must be supplied at a predetermined rate. The SDR implementation therefore generates samples in vectorised form. Before transmission begins, the complete packet is read and every input bit is mapped to a value of $\beta$.

In this implementation, information is encoded only through the asymmetry parameter $\beta$. Let $M_\beta$ denote the number of possible values in its mapping table. One symbol then represents $\log_2(M_\beta)$ bits. Since every symbol contains $N$ samples, transmitting $B$ bits requires $\frac{BN}{\log_2(M_\beta)}$ samples. For the binary mapping $\beta\in\{-1,1\}$, $M_\beta=2$, so every symbol represents one bit and $B$ bits require $BN$ samples.
 
The parameter vector is obtained by repeating the value of $\beta$ assigned to each symbol $N$ times. Independent values $V_i \sim \mathcal{U}\left(-\frac{\pi}{2},\frac{\pi}{2}\right)$ and $W_i \sim \operatorname{Exp}(1)$ are then generated, with $i$ running from $0$ to $N-1$. Every sample therefore has independent $V_i$ and $W_i$, while all samples belonging to one symbol share the same $\beta$. The samples are calculated using the CMS method in Equation~\ref{CMS}.

This does not make the samples of a symbol identical. Only $\beta$ is shared; new random values $V_i$ and $W_i$ are used for every sample. Each sample is consequently an independent realisation of the selected $\alpha$-stable distribution.

All samples are therefore produced in one vectorised operation. The complete transmission frame is then assembled and stored in the output buffer. GNU Radio can feed these precomputed samples gradually to the SDR without performing computationally expensive random-variable generation during transmission, substantially reducing the probability of underflow.

\subsection{Synchronisation}
Synchronising an $\alpha$-stable signal is difficult because it resembles noise. Searching for a packet start directly in the $\alpha$-stable samples would therefore produce many false detections, as the receiver continuously observes noise and other interference even when the transmitter is idle.

A deterministic synchronisation sequence is therefore added to the beginning of every packet. The current sequence contains 32 bits, each represented by $N$ samples. This permits more reliable correlation-based detection in noise. The transmitter generates the sequence in advance, while the receiver knows the same reference and searches for it in the incoming signal.

The receiver slides the reference sequence along the input stream and calculates signal correlation and coherence. A candidate is accepted only when both exceed their configured thresholds, reducing the probability that random noise is interpreted as a packet start.

Thus, before every packet, the transmitter sends a predefined deterministic sequence that allows the receiver to detect the packet and determine its starting position.

\subsection{Pilot Symbols and Carrier Tracking}

Initial synchronisation determines the packet start and provides an initial carrier estimate, but this estimate is insufficiently accurate over a long packet. The transmitter and receiver use separate local oscillators, causing frequency and phase offsets, and the phase may also drift slowly during a packet.

The carrier phase cannot be estimated reliably from CMS-generated $\alpha$-stable samples because they are too random. A deterministic pilot symbol is therefore placed before each $\alpha$-stable data symbol.

For a given $N$, every bit is transmitted as $N$ pilot samples followed by $N$ $\alpha$-stable samples. The pilot is always the same and carries no payload. Before subcarrier modulation, it consists of $N$ samples with constant positive amplitude. The receiver estimates the local frequency offset from consecutive pilot samples and then the local phase offset from the mean phase of the frequency-corrected pilot. The resulting estimate corrects only the $\alpha$-stable symbol immediately following that pilot.

This is more robust than using a single frequency and phase estimate for an entire packet because each data symbol receives a local carrier reference. The pilot does not resynchronise the packet or identify a new packet start; its sole purpose is local frequency- and phase-offset correction.

\subsection{Clock-Offset Correction}

Carrier frequency and phase offsets are not the only issues; the transmitter and receiver also have separate clocks. Although both devices are configured for the same nominal sampling rate, their actual rates differ slightly. This error accumulates over a packet, so the final symbols of a long packet may shift by several hundred samples, making communication unusable.

A pilot alone does not remove the sampling-clock offset. The receiver therefore estimates the actual length of a pilot--data pair from the first transition between pilot and data and the transition between the last data symbol and the packet end. Based on this estimate, interpolation converts every pilot and data symbol to $N$ samples. The method assumes that timing drift changes little during a packet or, if it changes, does so approximately linearly. A shared external reference clock would reduce this offset, but such equipment was not available for the experiment.


\chapter{Evaluation in the Simulation Environment}

This chapter evaluates the developed communication system in the GNU Radio simulation environment. It analyses how the number of segments $L$, the difference between the two values of $\beta$, and the scale parameter $\gamma$ affect the bit error rate. The final section presents results obtained with SDR hardware.

All simulations used the system shown in Figure~\ref{GNU}. The data source generated a random binary sequence, which the encoder converted into $\alpha$-stable samples. The signal passed through a simulated channel, after which the receiver estimated $\beta$ and reconstructed the transmitted bits. The stability parameter was fixed at $\alpha=1.1$ in every experiment; the remaining settings are stated with the individual experiments.

Each configuration was evaluated in 100 independent repetitions, with 1024 randomly generated bits transmitted per repetition. Thus, every configuration was evaluated over $102\,400$ bits. BER was calculated as the ratio of incorrectly decoded bits to all transmitted bits.

Because $\mathrm{BER}=0$ cannot be shown on a logarithmic axis, configurations with no detected errors are plotted at
\[
\mathrm{BER}=\frac{1}{102\,400}\approx10^{-5}.
\]
This is the lower plotting limit and does not mean that one error was actually observed.

The simulation parameters are described before the results.

\section{Effect of the Number of Segments $L$}

To estimate $\beta$, the decoder divides the $N$ samples of a symbol into $L$ equally sized segments, each containing $K=N/L$ samples. This experiment compared $L\in\{2,4,5,8,10,20\}$ for different values of $N$. The mapping was $\beta\in\{-1,1\}$, with $\gamma=1$ and \texttt{noise\_ratio}$=1$.

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{l_error_log.png}
  \caption{BER as a function of samples per symbol $N$ for different values of $L$.}
  \label{fig:l_segments}
\end{figure}

For every tested value of $L$, BER decreases as the number of samples per symbol increases. The decoder with $L=2$ achieved the lowest BER throughout the observed range, whereas progressively larger values of $L$ required more samples to attain comparable reliability.

The difference is particularly pronounced at $N=400$. With $L=2$, the BER was approximately $6.8\cdot10^{-5}$, corresponding to 7 errors among $102\,400$ transmitted bits. At $L=8$, it was approximately $9.4\cdot10^{-3}$, or 963 errors---about 138 times as many as for $L=2$. At $L=20$, the BER reached approximately $0.23$, corresponding to 23,414 errors, or about 3345 times as many as for $L=2$. These absolute error counts make the difference, which is less obvious on a logarithmic plot, more tangible. No incorrectly decoded bits were observed for $L=2$, $L=4$, or $L=5$ at $N=600$, whereas for $L=20$ the BER remained approximately $1.3\cdot10^{-4}$ even at $N=1000$.


\section{Varying the Parameter $\beta$}

The parameter $\beta$ determines the asymmetry of an $\alpha$-stable distribution and is used to encode information in the developed system. The experiment used symmetric mappings ranging from $\{-0.1,0.1\}$ to $\{-1,1\}$, with the difference defined as $\Delta\beta=\beta_1-\beta_0$. A smaller $\Delta\beta$ means that the two bit distributions are more similar and therefore harder to distinguish. The difference may also be regarded as a security parameter because greater similarity makes decoding more difficult both for the intended receiver and for a potential eavesdropper.

The simulation used $L=2$, $\gamma=1$, and no additional Gaussian noise. The values $N\in\{40,80,160,240,320,400,600,800,1000\}$ were compared.

\begin{figure}[h]
\begin{center}
\includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{beta_error.png}
\end{center}
\caption{BER as a function of $\Delta\beta$ for different values of $N$.}
\label{beta_error}
\end{figure}

The results show that BER decreases both as $\Delta\beta$ and as the number of samples per symbol increase. For small differences, the two distributions are similar, so the estimated values of $\beta$ can overlap and the receiver often selects the wrong symbol.

The sharpest BER decrease occurs at $\Delta\beta=2$, corresponding to $\beta\in\{-1,1\}$. At these extreme values, the distribution for $\beta=-1$ is strongly asymmetric in one direction and that for $\beta=1$ in the opposite direction. The regions containing their characteristic extremes therefore overlap very little. Since the receiver estimates $\beta$ precisely from segment maxima and minima, the two symbol estimates are well separated under this mapping and the receiver's decision is substantially easier.

The sample count remains important even at the largest separation. At $\Delta\beta=2$, BER decreased from approximately $0.39$ at $N=40$ to approximately $1.4\cdot10^{-4}$ at $N=320$. No bit errors were observed for $N\geq600$ in the completed repetitions.

\section{Effect of the Parameter $\gamma$}

This experiment analysed how $\gamma$ and the number of samples per symbol $N$ affect BER. The parameter $\gamma$ controls the spread of the $\alpha$-stable distribution and therefore the ratio between the information-signal power and the added Gaussian noise. The mapping was $\beta\in\{-1,1\}$, with $L=2$ and \texttt{noise\_ratio}$=1.0$. The values $N\in\{40,80,160,240,320,400,600,800,1000\}$ were compared.

MSNR was expressed in decibels as

\begin{equation}
\mathrm{MSNR}_{\mathrm{dB}} = 10 \log_{10}\!\left(\frac{\gamma}{\gamma_G}\right),
\end{equation}

where $\gamma_G=1$ is the scale parameter of the added Gaussian noise.

\begin{figure}[H]
\begin{center}
\includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{gama_error_log.png}
\end{center}
\caption{BER as a function of MSNR for different values of $N$.}
\label{gama_error}
\end{figure}

The results show that BER decreases as both MSNR and the number of samples per symbol increase. The effect of $N$ is particularly pronounced in noisy conditions. At $\mathrm{MSNR}=-10\,\mathrm{dB}$, BER was approximately $0.62$ for $N=40$, $1.8\cdot10^{-2}$ for $N=400$, $5.0\cdot10^{-4}$ for $N=800$, and only $2.9\cdot10^{-5}$ for $N=1000$. In the final case, only 3 of $102\,400$ transmitted bits were decoded incorrectly. Even at $\mathrm{MSNR}=-14\,\mathrm{dB}$, $N=1000$ achieved a BER of approximately $1.1\cdot10^{-2}$.

This behaviour indicates that the system can operate in considerably noisy environments. With a suitable channel model and adaptation rule, $N$ could be selected according to the estimated noise level. When channel conditions deteriorate, the transmitter could increase the sample count to give the receiver more observations for estimating $\beta$; when conditions improve, $N$ could be reduced again.

Such adaptation is relatively simple to implement because the mapping of $\beta$ and the estimation procedure remain unchanged; primarily the number of generated and processed samples per symbol must change. Conventional adaptive systems often respond to changing conditions by switching among modulation and coding schemes, whereas the proposed system can retain its basic encoding method and adjust only $N$.

Increasing the sample count also has a direct cost. At a fixed sampling rate, symbol duration grows in proportion to $N$, increasing transmission time and reducing the data rate. For the binary mapping used here, in which one symbol represents one bit,

\begin{equation}
T_s=\frac{N}{f_s},
\qquad
R_b=\frac{f_s}{N},
\end{equation}

where $f_s$ is the sampling rate, $T_s$ is the symbol duration, and $R_b$ is the bit rate. Selecting $N$ therefore involves a trade-off between noise resistance and temporal efficiency, illustrated more intuitively below.

\begin{tcolorbox}[colback=purple!5!white,colframe=purple!50!black,
title=Example: effect of sample count on transmission rate]
At a sampling rate of $f_s=32\,000$ samples/s and $N=1000$,
\[
T_s=\frac{1000}{32\,000}=31{,}25\,\mathrm{ms},
\qquad
R_b=\frac{32\,000}{1000}=32\,\mathrm{bit/s}.
\]
If $N$ is doubled to 2000, the symbol duration doubles to $62.5\,\mathrm{ms}$ and the bit rate halves to $16\,\mathrm{bit/s}$.
\end{tcolorbox}

\section{Results on SDR Hardware}

Measurements on SDR hardware showed that data transmission using $\alpha$-stable symbols is feasible. Communication was tested by modulating the asymmetry parameter with $\beta=[-1.0,1.0]$.

Measurements used symbol lengths of $N=100$, $N=500$, and $N=1000$ samples. In all measurements with $N\geq100$, the raw BER was below 1~\%, except for packets for which the receiver estimated a particularly large timing offset. Results were unreliable for $N<50$, and at $N=24$ the receiver could no longer detect the synchronisation sequence reliably, primarily because fewer samples were available for correlation detection.

After decoding, the receiver reports the estimated total timing offset in samples as \texttt{drift}. A large absolute value may indicate a mismatch between the sampling clocks, lost samples caused by \emph{underflow} or \emph{overflow}, or an incorrectly detected packet end. In such cases, the estimated pilot--data pair length is incorrect and the timing error grows over the packet. The first part may therefore decode correctly while BER increases sharply after a certain point.

The results confirm that communication by modulating $\beta$ of an $\alpha$-stable distribution is possible, although reliability depends strongly on synchronisation quality, correct timing-offset estimation, and uninterrupted sample delivery to the SDR. A shared external reference clock would reduce both carrier-frequency and sampling-clock offsets. Underflow and overflow should be further mitigated through suitable buffering and sufficiently fast sample generation and processing. Although these events were no longer observed during measurements with the latest implementation, they cannot be ruled out entirely, especially on less powerful computers or under more demanding operating conditions.
